Grain Size Analysis in Mullingar: Sieve and Hydrometer Testing for Smarter Earthworks

Practical geotechnics, field-tested.

LEARN MORE

Too many groundworks contracts in the Mullingar area stall because the imported fill doesn’t drain the way the spec assumed—water ponds, frost heave shows up, and the engineer flags the material. The root cause is almost always a missing or incomplete particle size curve. A proper grain size analysis that pairs mechanical sieving with a hydrometer test gives you the full distribution from coarse gravel down to colloidal clay. That one curve determines compaction behaviour, permeability, frost susceptibility, and whether the material can stay within the grading envelope laid down in the NRA Specification for Road Works. Before you commit to a borrow source near Lough Owel or open a pit on your own site, run the combined test; rehandling rejected fill costs far more than the lab work. For projects where the subgrade is borderline, we often recommend pairing the analysis with a CBR road test to confirm achievable bearing values under the pavement design.

A single combined sieve-and-hydrometer curve replaces three rounds of site arguments about whether the fill is 'too clayey' or 'too silty'—it gives you the numbers the spec actually demands.

Process overview

Mullingar sits at roughly 100 metres above sea level on undulating limestone till with scattered pockets of lacustrine clay left by the post-glacial lakes that once covered much of the Westmeath lowlands. These clay pockets contain a high proportion of silt and fine sand, and their grading curve often plots right in the frost-susceptible zone defined by Transport Infrastructure Ireland. A grain size analysis that stops at the 63-micron sieve misses the shape of the clay fraction entirely, which is where the hydrometer step becomes critical. By running the full IS EN ISO 17892-4:2016 procedure—wet sieving, dry sieving, and sedimentation—we deliver a curve that lets your designer nail the drainage blanket thickness, decide whether lime stabilisation is needed, and satisfy the earthworks acceptance criteria without arguments on site. When the grading suggests a gap-graded material that may not compact easily, we suggest looking at Proctor tests to establish a realistic density target for the specific borrow source.

Local context

The glacial tills around Mullingar can deliver a nasty surprise: a clean-looking sandy gravel that contains 15–20% fines, right on the borderline between free-draining and moisture-sensitive. If you classify it by eye and treat it as granular fill, the first winter rain saturates the fines, and the compacted layer loses stiffness exactly when the structural asphalt is going down. A full grain size analysis catches that borderline grading and forces the right call—either blend it with a coarser fraction or accept that it needs a drainage blanket beneath the capping layer. The opposite risk shows up in the low-lying land south of the Royal Canal, where lacustrine silts can plot as a near-vertical grading curve, meaning uniform pore size and high frost heave potential. The hydrometer data tells you whether the fines are silt or true clay, which completely changes the stabilisation strategy.

Q&A

What does a combined grain size analysis in Mullingar typically cost?

For a standard combined sieve-and-hydrometer test on a single sample, the fee ranges from €100 to €150. The exact price depends on whether the sample needs extended wet preparation, the number of sieve sizes requested, and how quickly you need the report. Batch pricing is available for site investigation campaigns with multiple samples.

How long does the hydrometer part of the test take?

The sedimentation phase requires readings over at least 24 hours, sometimes extending to 48 hours for very fine clays, because the settling rate follows Stokes’ law and the smallest particles need that time to drop. The sieving portion can usually be completed the same day, so a typical combined report is issued within three to five working days after the sample arrives at the lab.

Do we need the hydrometer if we are only placing structural fill under a building?

If the fill specification sets a maximum fines content—often 10% or 15% passing the 63 µm sieve—the hydrometer is the only way to check compliance. The wet-sieving stage tells you the total passing 63 µm, but the hydrometer splits that into silt and clay, which matters because a high clay fraction can cause long-term settlement even in a compacted fill.
