Rigid Pavement Design in Mullingar: Concrete Roads That Last

Practical geotechnics, field-tested.

LEARN MORE

Too many industrial yards in Mullingar crack within five years. The problem is rarely the concrete mix—it’s the sub-base not matching the local ground. We see this pattern across Westmeath, where drumlin-derived clays sit over limestone till. One contractor on the N4 business park lost half their slab area to settlement cracks. The fix was a redesigned granular layer and tighter joint spacing. A proper CBR test for roads at formation level would have caught the weak spot before the pour. Rigid pavement design here is not about standard details; it is about reading the soil’s memory and giving the slab a base that breathes without moving.

A rigid pavement in Mullingar lives or dies by its joint detailing and its sub-base—get those two right and the slab will outlast the building it serves.

Process overview

A distribution centre off the Castlepollard Road needed a jointed plain concrete pavement that could handle 40-tonne articulated trucks turning daily. The subgrade was a stiff boulder clay that looked solid in the dry summer but turned spongy each winter. The design reduced transverse joint spacing to 3.6 metres and specified a 200 mm cement-bound granular layer beneath the slab. Edge thickening at the loading bays managed the high corner stresses without switching to a fully reinforced slab. Load transfer across joints relied on dowel bars at 300 mm centres, sized per the mid-lane stress predictions from the Westergaard equations. Tie bars along the longitudinal joints kept lane separation under control. This is the kind of detail that stops a perfectly poured slab from becoming a patching project in year three.

Local context

Mullingar sits at roughly 100 metres elevation in the Brosna River catchment, with groundwater perched high in many areas during winter and spring. This shallow water table is the enemy of a rigid pavement. Water trapped in the sub-base fines creates pumping at the joints; every truck pass ejects a little more soil from under the slab edge. Over time, voids form. The slab loses support. Cracks follow. In our experience on Westmeath industrial estates, the worst failures occur where the drainage layer was omitted to save cost. A clean, open-graded drainage layer and positive crossfall are not optional here—they are survival features. Dowel bar retrofitting can help, but it is expensive and disruptive. Getting the hydraulic regime right at design stage is far cheaper.

Q&A

What thickness of concrete slab do I need for a yard with articulated trucks in Mullingar?

For a jointed plain concrete pavement carrying daily 40-tonne articulated trucks on a CBR 6–8 subgrade, we typically specify 200–220 mm slab thickness. This assumes a minimum 150 mm cement-bound sub-base and short joint spacing. Heavier or more frequent loading may push the design to 250 mm. The exact number comes from the Westergaard analysis tied to your specific ground conditions.

How much does rigid pavement design cost for a commercial project in Westmeath?

Design fees for a rigid pavement in the Mullingar area generally range from €1,680 to €5,000, depending on the yard size, traffic loading complexity, and the number of subgrade investigation points required. A modest industrial yard with standard truck traffic and clear ground data will sit at the lower end; a large distribution hub with multiple loading zones and difficult ground will be at the upper end.

Why do concrete slabs crack so quickly in this part of Westmeath?

The main culprit is water management. Mullingar’s drumlin clays hold moisture, and the water table rises close to the surface in winter. Without a proper drainage layer, water saturates the sub-base fines and pumps out at the joints under traffic loads. The slab loses support, voids form, and cracks develop. Short panel lengths, solid sub-base drainage, and well-sealed joints stop this cycle.
